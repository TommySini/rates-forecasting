Fed Decision Forecasting

Can a model that understands the economy predict whether the Federal Reserve will cut, hold or hike, and ever do better than prediction markets?

  • Best modelA five-number model reading the Fed's last move and Treasury yield spreads. It beats every larger model we tried on the same test meetings.
  • MarketsOne week before a decision, Kalshi and Polymarket are still about ten times more accurate.
  • NextForecast one to three months ahead, where markets are less certain, and bring in fed funds futures back to 1988.

Code and detailed reports: github.com/TommySini/rates-forecasting. All results are exploratory research, not trading advice.

What exists

The data and the rules

207scheduled Fed meetings, Feb 2000 to Dec 2025
27cuts among them, with 40 hikes and 140 holds
26test meetings, Nov 2022 to Dec 2025
7 daysahead: each forecast is made at 6 p.m. New York time a week before the decision

Every forecast uses only what was known at that moment. Economic data comes from archives of past data versions, so a revised jobs number never leaks into an earlier forecast. Treasury yields before mid-2005 are taken as never revised; spot checks match the Fed's original 2001 releases exactly. Each model is refit before every meeting using only decisions already announced, and every experiment's success test was written down before its results were computed.

Test meetings: 14 holds 6 hikes 6 cuts
Results

How the models score

Scores use the Brier score: the squared gap between the probabilities given and what happened, averaged over meetings. Lower is better. Zero is perfect; always guessing the historical mix of decisions scores 0.637.

Against prediction markets

Market prices were taken at the same moment as our forecasts, never later. The gap is about information, not timing. By a week out the Fed has stopped speaking publicly (its quiet period starts about eleven days before each decision), and traders have already priced its speeches, minutes, press conferences and the news. Our models see only economic data, yields and past decisions.

Findings

What we learned

  • Small models win. With 207 meetings, models that learn a handful of numbers beat models that learn dozens. Tuning pushed every flexible model toward its most restricted settings.
  • The Taylor rule was not the reason. Deliberately wrong economic starting values scored the same. The gain came from the Fed's habit of repeating its last move, which larger models had smoothed away.
  • Yields are the strongest signal so far, especially before turning points. Much of that is the market's own forecast built into short-term yields.
  • The 1-month bill added noise. During the May 2023 debt-ceiling standoff it priced a cut that never came, which caused most of its loss.
  • A first economy model forecast economic data better than simple rules, but that did not carry over to Fed decisions.
Open problems

Where we are stuck

  • One week ahead, the answer is mostly public. Beating markets at this horizon with economic data alone is not realistic.
  • Few events. 27 cuts and about ten genuine turning points since 2000. The 26 test meetings have been reused across experiments, so small differences are not proof.
  • The best long-history market data is not in hand yet. Fed funds futures price every meeting back to 1988 but need a licensed source.
Plan

Next steps

  1. Get fed funds futures, 1988 to 2025

    Data request

    Daily prices for every contract month through Stanford's licensed data services. This gives a market forecast for about 300 meetings at any horizon.

  2. Forecast at 1 week, 1, 2 and 3 months

    Modeling

    Rebuild the yield-spread model at each distance from the meeting to see where markets are still unsure. That is the only place an economic edge can exist.

  3. Start from the market, correct with the economy

    Modeling

    Use the market forecast as the baseline and let economic trends make a small adjustment. With no adjustment it equals the market, so it cannot do worse.

  4. Swap in futures when they arrive

    Modeling

    Convert futures prices to meeting probabilities with the method behind CME's FedWatch tool, check them against Kalshi, and rerun every horizon.

  5. A self-learning economy model

    World model

    Learns trends from about 128 monthly economic series back to 1959 by predicting next month's data, then feeds a small Fed model. Built only for horizons where step 3 shows room.

  6. Pre-train on simulated economies

    World model

    Thousands of simulated booms, recessions and turning points to make up for how few real ones exist, tested against a deliberately wrong simulator.

  7. Keep a live forecast log

    Ongoing

    Record a forecast before every upcoming Fed meeting. Future meetings are the only clean test left.

Reference

Terms used here

Brier score
Accuracy of probability forecasts. For each meeting, add up the squared differences between the probabilities given to cut, hold and hike and what happened, then average. Lower is better, from 0 to 2.
Turning point
A move that ends a pause: the first cut or hike after one or more holds.
Yield spread
A Treasury yield minus the Fed's current rate. When short-term yields sit below the Fed's rate, markets expect cuts.
Point-in-time
Using each number as it was known on the forecast date, before later revisions.
Fed funds futures
Exchange-traded contracts that pay out based on the average Fed rate in a month. Their prices imply the market's odds for each meeting.
